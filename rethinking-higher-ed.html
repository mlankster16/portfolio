A required course that meets learners where they are

Students arrive in a required science course with different interests, prior knowledge, and reasons for being there. I’d like to give them more ways to connect with the material while helping everyone build the course’s core knowledge and skills.

We now have the technology and resources to make that possible. AI can start from what motivates a learner, why they’re in this course or program, and what kind of experience they’re hoping for, then build the concepts and skills of a STEM curriculum around those goals.

What varies

  • The examples that make it relevant
  • The preparation each learner needs
  • How they choose to begin
+

What stays the same

  • The scientific question
  • The core knowledge and skills
  • The shared assessment

In this concept, learners can approach the same scientific question through different examples and activities. Their choices help shape the experience, and shared assessments let us examine what they can do with what they’ve learned.

From syllabus topics to questions learners can investigate

I’d begin with the course’s existing syllabus and approved learning outcomes. Then I’d look for questions that bring related topics together and give learners a reason to explore them.

For example, evolution, animal behavior, and ecology could come together around a question about how organisms respond to environmental change. Learners would still study the underlying biology, but they would use it to investigate a question and explain what the evidence suggests.

I’d map the activities and assessments back to the approved outcomes to check that the redesigned course addresses the required knowledge and skills.

Before the course begins, I’d ask learners about their interests, prior knowledge, and reasons for taking it. Their answers would help suggest a starting point, with room to change direction as they learn.

    Activity formats

    Each activity would give learners practice interpreting evidence and explaining what it suggests. They could choose an activity and try another if they needed a different approach.

    I’d compare their work and feedback to understand where each activity helped and where it needed improvement.

    All three investigate

    How does birdsong differ between a city park and a nearby forest, and why?

    What learners do
    Compare recordings and spectrograms, make predictions, and examine an expert’s reasoning.
    Prepares for the shared task
    Shows how an expert reads the evidence and builds an explanation before learners write their own.
    What learners do
    Use recordings, noise measurements, and field notes to evaluate competing explanations.
    Prepares for the shared task
    Practices weighing another possible explanation, with feedback on each puzzle before the next one unlocks.
    What learners do
    Collect a local recording and compare it with supplied recordings and noise data. Learners who can’t record outdoors can use a supplied local recording instead.
    Prepares for the shared task
    Works with real, imperfect data and identifies what additional evidence would help.

    Over time, the course would track which paths learners choose and how they do. If one format is more popular, it would be refined, and new ideas would keep getting tested alongside it.

    Preparation

    Learners can use a short refresher before beginning the investigation. For this unit, that might mean practice reading a spectrogram or interpreting a graph. A brief check would help them decide whether they need more preparation or are ready to continue.

    Reading and comparing graphsFrequency and pitchReading a spectrogramWeighing evidence for different explanations

    Motivation

    Some learners arrive with strong intrinsic motivation. Others need extra support and outside encouragement to keep going. Both are okay, and good design can support each of them.

    Game elements like points, streaks, and progress maps can help a learner build momentum early. The goal is to shift the reward over the term, from showing up toward what a learner can now do and explain, so the motivation becomes their own.

    Outside encouragementTheir own reasons
    1. Weeks 1–3Points and streaks for finishing checkpoints
    2. Weeks 4–7Badges tied to skills, not activity
    3. Weeks 8–11Learners pick their own challenges
    4. Weeks 12–15An independent project on a question they choose

    Make the learning visible

    Each activity would prepare learners for the same final task. Shared criteria would make the expectations clear while allowing learners to draw on evidence from their chosen investigation.

    Guided video: checkpoint predictions and notes
    Escape room: a log weighing explanations
    Field investigation: a local recording compared with supplied data

    Your end of unit goal

    Use the recordings and environmental data to explain a difference in birdsong between two settings. Consider another possible explanation and identify what additional evidence would help you investigate it.
    Uses evidenceDraws on the recordings and environmental data
    Interprets dataReads patterns in spectrograms and noise data accurately
    Considers another explanationWeighs a competing idea fairly
    Identifies further evidenceNames what would help test it

    Feedback throughout the unit uses the same criteria as the final task. Checkpoints help learners see what they’re doing well and where to focus next. They also help faculty identify where learners may need more support.
