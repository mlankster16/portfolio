Project Management

How I scope, kick off, manage, deliver, and evaluate projects for Duke initiatives.

The projects I lead range from multi-course Coursera Specializations to faculty-built courses and interactive learning tools. Each one moves through the same five phases, and I choose a delivery approach based on what we’re building and who we’re building it with.

The project lifecycle

Every project I manage moves through five phases. The approach changes what happens inside each phase, especially during development, but the phases stay the same.

  1. Scope

    Define goals, audience, deliverables, and constraints with partners.

    What this includes
    • Scoping conversations with faculty and stakeholders
    • Effort, budget, and timeline estimates
    • Scope of Work and sign-off

  2. Kickoff

    Align the team on roles, schedule, and how we’ll work together.

    What this includes
    • Kickoff meeting and shared project plan
    • Roles and responsibilities
    • Communication cadence and tools

  3. Manage

    Keep development on track while the work takes shape.

    What this includes
    • Status check-ins and progress tracking
    • Risk, issue, and change management
    • Review cycles with partners

  4. Deliver

    Launch, hand off, and capture what we learned.

    What this includes
    • Quality and accessibility review
    • Launch and handoff
    • Retrospective and lessons learned

  5. Evaluate

    Review how the product is working and plan what comes next.

    What this includes
    • Learner feedback
    • Backend analytics
    • Next steps for improvement and optimization

Choosing an approach

I choose an approach during scoping, based on how well-defined the work is, how often partners can review it, and how much the product is likely to change as we build.

Agile

Best when:

  • The product will change as we learn
  • Partners can give frequent feedback
  • Working versions matter more than a full spec
See the cycle ↓

Hybrid

Best when:

  • The plan is set but details will evolve
  • Partners review work in batches
  • Some pieces need room to iterate
See the cycle ↓

Traditional (Waterfall)

Best when:

  • Requirements are clear from the start
  • Deadlines and approvals are fixed
  • Each phase builds on the one before

Agile

Work is planned and delivered in short sprints, with a working version reviewed at the end of each one.

↻ Repeats each sprint

Product & backlog

Each product starts with a short definition: what it is, who it’s for, the problem it solves, and what success looks like at MVP. The owner then builds a backlog of cards sorted into Discovery, Build, Test, Refine, Deploy, and Icebox.

Output: a product definition and a prioritized backlog on our Monday.com board

Sprint planning

At the start of each one-week sprint, each person pulls two to four ready cards from their backlog and writes a one-sentence sprint goal. The goal stays fixed for the week. The cards underneath it can be swapped, split, or added to.

Output: a sprint goal, committed cards, and estimated hours

Build & check in

Midway through the sprint, everyone posts a short async update: progress toward their goal, any blocked cards, and what support they need. Blockers come up here instead of waiting for the review.

Output: an async update in Teams, with blocked cards flagged on the board

Review & demo

At the end of each sprint, each person shows what they built or learned and reflects on whether they met their goal. Work can be rough; the point is to make progress visible to the team.

Output: goal met, partial, or not met, plus cards carried into the next sprint

Retrospective

Right after the review, a 15-minute conversation about how we worked rather than what we built: what went well, what was hard, and what we’ll change next sprint.

Output: one or two adjustments for the next sprint, and blockers escalated to leadership

Example project

AI Pilot

Over a summer, four members of Duke’s Learning Experience Design team each built AI-powered tools for our course development work. It was also the team’s first Agile project, so we tested a new way of working alongside a new set of tools.

Timeline
June to August 2026, in one-week sprints
My role
Designed and facilitated the Agile process; product owner for two of the tools
Why Agile
We didn’t yet know what the tools could do. Weekly cycles let each person test, learn, and adjust, and kept everyone’s progress visible.

How it played out

  • Goals held while plans flexed. Capacity shifted week to week with time off and other projects. Each person kept a sprint goal and adjusted the cards beneath it, so lighter weeks still moved the work forward.
  • Blockers surfaced weekly. Hosting, API budget, and admin approvals came up at check-ins and went to leadership as one shared list, which became each tool’s path to production.
  • Learning was shared. Weekly demos let us see how each person was approaching prompts, agents, and hosting. When a tool reached MVP, its owner picked up the next priority from our shared list.

What we built

  • Coursera interaction generator
  • Claude Skills library and dashboard
  • Capacity forecasting model
  • Course design outline tool
  • Script writing agent

Hybrid

The overall roadmap follows a fixed sequence of phases and sign-off milestones. Within it, the design work moves through scheduled cycles of review, feedback, and implementation.

↻ Review, feedback, implement

Kickoff & discovery

A project kickoff and onboarding for each faculty lead, followed by one-on-one walkthroughs with each member of the design team to discuss the course, its learning goals, and what it should include.

Milestone: onboarding and course walkthroughs complete, with faculty sign-off

Design & alignment

Faculty leads develop their course design with the design team through ongoing planning meetings. Full project team meetings bring every course together for alignment, and the gaps or open questions they surface shape the next co-design session.

Milestone: course design drafted, with faculty sign-off

Co-design

Practicing nurses respond to the questions and gaps identified during design. Faculty leads and the design team refine each course from that input before production begins.

Milestone: course and module maps finalized, and every learning asset identified

Production & build

Scripting, studio recording, video editing, and course build. Scripts and video edits each get a dedicated review round, and a second co-design session gathers feedback across all courses near completion.

Milestone: video edits approved and all courses built and ready for review

Review & launch

Continuing education review, stakeholder feedback, launch preparation, and submission to Coursera.

Milestone: courses launch-ready and the Specialization launched

Example project · In development

Ethics, Equity, and Patient Safety in AI for Nursing Practice

A four-course Coursera Specialization for practicing nurses, developed with Duke University School of Nursing as Year 2 of the Empowering Nurses initiative with Johnson & Johnson. Year 1 was AI for Nurses. Each course has its own faculty lead, supported by one shared CTL design team.

Timeline
September 2026 to an August 2027 launch
My role
Project manager and senior learning experience designer across all four courses
Why Hybrid
A fixed launch date, continuing education credit, and four courses developed in parallel call for a dependable roadmap. Feedback from practicing nurses shapes the design, so co-design sessions are scheduled into it.

How it’s structured

  • Sign-offs keep courses in step. Faculty lead sign-offs at key milestones, from the finalized course design to launch-ready courses, move all four courses into each new phase together.
  • Co-design with practicing nurses. Two sessions, one during design and one near completion. The School of Nursing recruits nurses, and we plan each session around gaps surfaced in faculty design meetings.
  • Review rounds are built in. Scripts and video edits each get a dedicated review with the design team, and full team meetings keep the four courses aligned across the Specialization.

The four courses

  • Ethical Foundations of AI in Nursing Practice
  • Bias, Fairness, and Health Equity in AI-Enabled Care
  • Understanding AI Tools, Data, and Technical Limits
  • Patient Safety, Simulation, and Responsible Generative AI Use

Traditional (Waterfall)

Work moves through defined phases in order, with each phase reviewed and approved before the next begins.

Initiate

Meetings with faculty and leadership define the project’s scope, objectives, and success criteria, and identify everyone who has a stake in the outcome.

Milestone: project charter and stakeholder register approved

Plan

An integrated project plan sets the scope, schedule, and cost baselines, supported by a work breakdown structure, a communication plan, and a risk register.

Milestone: baselines approved, with milestones for drafts, media production, and launch

Develop

The team designs course materials, produces video, and builds assignments in sequence. Progress is tracked against the baselines, and any change goes through a formal change request.

Milestone: course materials, videos, and assignments complete

Review & QA

Courses are checked against accessibility and quality standards, then reviewed by stakeholders before launch.

Milestone: stakeholder acceptance of the courses

Launch & close

Courses launch, lessons learned are documented, project files are archived, and ownership moves to the team that maintains the courses.

Milestone: launch and handoff for ongoing maintenance

Example project · Launched

Data Science with R

A four-course Coursera Specialization developed with Duke’s Department of Statistics, taught through video lectures, code-along exercises, and graded programming assignments.

Timeline
February to December 2024
My role
Project manager for a design team of three, working with two faculty instructors and four additional stakeholders
Why Traditional
The scope was clear from the start: four courses with defined lessons and assessments. Fixed baselines made progress easy to track and changes easy to manage.

How it played out

  • Baselines set the path. Scope, schedule, and cost baselines and a work breakdown structure set milestones for drafts, media production, and launch.
  • A pause then a rebaseline. When faculty availability paused development mid-year, we moved through a formal change request, reallocated resources, and rebaselined the schedule once work resumed.
  • On budget with a clean handoff. The Specialization launched three months later than first planned and within budget, then moved to our Technical Support team for ongoing maintenance.

The four courses

  • Data Visualization and Transformation with R
  • Data Tidying and Importing with R
  • Data Science Ethics with R
  • Data Modeling and Prediction with R
