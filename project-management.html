01 · The lifecycle

A flexible approach to each project

This is the general flow I follow, but it’s not a fixed sequence. The time and attention each phase needs depend on the project and the people involved. Phases often overlap, and on Agile projects, we revisit them in short cycles.
01
ScopeAgree with partners on who we’re serving, what we’re trying to accomplish, what we’ll deliver, and what’s outside the scope.
02
KickoffGet everyone together (in person or virtually) to clarify roles, timing, and how we’ll work with one another.
03
ManageKeep the work moving, make room for feedback, and work through decisions and obstacles as they come up.
04
DeliverLaunch or hand off the work, make sure people have what they need, and capture what we learned.
05
EvaluateLook at how it’s working and decide what to improve or take on next.
02 · Choosing an approach

Matching the approach to the project

I start thinking about the approach during scoping. Two questions help: how much do we know up front, and how often can partners give feedback? I choose a way of working that fits those answers and adjust as we go. This is about how we get the work done together. For how I think through what learners do, see Learning Design.
AgileBest when
We expect the work to change as we learn.
Partners can give frequent feedback.
We need something we can try before planning every detail.
Example: AI Pilot
HybridBest when
The overall plan is set, but details will evolve.
Partners review work in batches rather than continuously.
Some parts need a clear sequence, while others need room to experiment.
Example: Ethics, Equity, and Patient Safety in AI for Nursing Practice
TraditionalBest when
Requirements are clear from the start.
Deadlines and approvals are fixed.
Each phase needs to be finished before the next can move ahead.
Example: Data Science with R
03 · Agile

Agile

We plan and build in short sprints, and look at a working version together at the end of each one.
↻ Repeats each sprint
↻ Repeats each sprint
Step 1 of 5Product and backlog

Each product starts with a short definition: what it is, who it’s for, the problem it solves, and what success looks like for a first usable version (the MVP). The Product Owner then builds a backlog of cards sorted into Discovery, Build, Test, Refine, Deploy, and Icebox.

Output: a product definition and a prioritized backlog on our Monday.com board

Step 2 of 5Sprint planning

At the start of each one-week sprint, each person pulls two to four ready cards from their backlog and writes a one-sentence sprint goal. The goal stays fixed for the week. The cards underneath it can be swapped, split, or added to.

Output: a sprint goal, committed cards, and estimated hours

Step 3 of 5Build and check in

Midway through the sprint, everyone posts a short async update: progress toward their goal, any blocked cards, and what support they need. Blockers come up here instead of waiting for the review.

Output: an async update in Teams, with blocked cards flagged on the board

Step 4 of 5Review and demo

At the end of each sprint, each person shows what they built or learned and reflects on whether they met their goal. Work can be rough. The point is to make progress visible.

Output: goal met, partial, or not met, plus cards carried into the next sprint

Step 5 of 5Retrospective

Right after the review, a 15-minute conversation about how we worked rather than what we built: what went well, what was hard, and what we’ll change next sprint.

Output: one or two adjustments for the next sprint, and blockers escalated to leadership

Inside the pilot ▾
The ask

Leadership asked our team to explore where AI could handle clearly defined steps in course development, giving designers more time for work that needs their judgment and expertise. We used that goal as a direction, not a checklist, and agreed on a few principles: a person stays responsible for every decision, bold experiments are welcome, and it’s okay to say, “This didn’t work.”

Coaching along the way

Several teammates had never built software. Alongside running the pilot, I coached them as they built. I asked who each tool was for and pushed on questions like, “How will it avoid sounding like AI?” That question led one colleague to build a library of strong example scripts. I also explained API keys and hosting in plain language and shared my own prompts and Claude Skills so others could adapt them.

Adjusting as we went

The task cards we drafted with AI were too detailed and hard to act on. We simplified them and kept the weekly show-and-tell sessions at the center of the process. When most of the team was out for a week, we shared updates asynchronously rather than skipping the sprint.

Results

Each tool started as a minimum viable product (MVP), a small working version we could test and build on. Three tools were working after the first one-week sprint. At the end of the pilot, I compiled a report for leadership showing each tool’s status and what it would need to be ready for regular use, including hosting and API costs. That helped leadership decide what to invest in.

04 · Hybrid

Hybrid

A set roadmap with sign-offs, plus planned rounds of review and feedback inside it.
↻ Review, feedback, implement
↻ Review, feedback, implement
Step 1 of 5Kickoff and discovery

A project kickoff and onboarding for each faculty lead, followed by one-on-one walkthroughs with each member of the design team to discuss the course, its learning goals, and what it should include.

Milestone: onboarding and course walkthroughs complete, with faculty sign-off

Step 2 of 5Design and alignment

Faculty leads develop their course design with the design team through ongoing planning meetings. Full project team meetings bring every course together for alignment, and the gaps or open questions they surface shape the next co-design session.

Milestone: course design drafted, with faculty sign-off

Step 3 of 5Co-design

Practicing nurses respond to the questions and gaps identified during design. Faculty leads and the design team refine each course from that input before production begins.

Milestone: course and module maps finalized, and every learning asset identified

Step 4 of 5Production and build

Scripting, studio recording, video editing, and course build. Scripts and video edits each get a dedicated review round, and a second co-design session gathers feedback across all courses near completion.

Milestone: video edits approved and all courses built and ready for review

Step 5 of 5Review and launch

Continuing education review, stakeholder feedback, launch preparation, and submission to Coursera.

Milestone: courses launch-ready and the Specialization launched

05 · Traditional

Traditional (Waterfall)

Work moves through set phases in order, and each one is approved before the next begins.
Step 1 of 5Initiate

Meetings with faculty and leadership define the project’s scope, objectives, and success criteria, and identify everyone who has a stake in the outcome.

Milestone: project charter and stakeholder register approved

Step 2 of 5Plan

An integrated project plan sets the scope, schedule, and cost baselines, supported by a work breakdown structure, a communication plan, and a risk register.

Milestone: baselines approved, with milestones for drafts, media production, and launch

Step 3 of 5Develop

The team designs course materials, produces video, and builds assignments in sequence. Progress is tracked against the baselines, and any change goes through a formal change request.

Milestone: course materials, videos, and assignments complete

Step 4 of 5Review and QA

Courses are checked against accessibility and quality standards, then reviewed by stakeholders before launch.

Milestone: stakeholder acceptance of the courses

Step 5 of 5Launch and close

Courses launch, lessons learned are documented, project files are archived, and ownership moves to the team that maintains the courses.

Milestone: launch and handoff for ongoing maintenance
