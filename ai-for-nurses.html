AI for Nurses

Helping nurses make sense of AI in their everyday work, and keep their own clinical judgment at the center of every decision.
Explore the action map ↓
At a glance

AI for Nurses is a three-course Coursera Specialization from the Duke University School of Nursing, designed for people across the nursing care team. I led the design and delivery with three faculty, with nurses helping shape the examples and activities along the way. It launched in September 2026. So far, learners who have finished rated it 5.0 out of 5, and our partners have funded a second year.

01 · A gap between AI learning and clinical practice

Making sense of AI on a nursing shift

Faculty at the Duke University School of Nursing were noticing a gap: nurses were being asked to use AI-enabled tools without much explanation of what they do or how to work with them. Sometimes a new tool appeared in their workflow with little introduction. Other times, AI was already part of the systems they used, but nurses had no reason to recognize it as AI.

Together with three School of Nursing faculty, we designed the series to give nurses a foundation for understanding and working with AI. It was the first of three planned Coursera Specializations.

02 · Co-designing with nurses

Learning where AI shows up across a real shift

During development, I designed and led a co-design session with registered nurses. They shared examples of how AI had shaped their recent shifts, reviewed our draft modules, and tried a demo of the AI-powered Dialogue activity. Four themes stood out and helped shape the Specialization.
1AI is part of the workflow, even when it isn’t called AI.Nurses described alerts, scores, and automated summaries as part of the electronic health record (EHR), rather than as separate AI tools. Many came with little explanation of how they worked.
2An alert is a starting point for assessment.Nurses described checking alerts against their own assessment of the patient. They felt that newer nurses might lean more heavily on a score, while experienced nurses might be more comfortable questioning it.
3The tool doesn’t see everything a nurse sees.Nonverbal cues, changes in behavior, and the patient’s surroundings can matter at the bedside without appearing in the data behind a risk score.
4A shorter note can leave out part of the story.Nurses saw the time-saving potential of AI-written summaries, but worried that important assessment details could get lost before reaching the rest of the care team.

Turning what we learned into competencies

After the session, I turned what nurses told us into competencies, the skills we wanted them to leave with. These competencies were the starting point for the action map in the next section, where each one becomes something a nurse does on a shift.

03 · Action map

Turning everyday AI moments into decision practice

I used Cathy Moore’s action mapping (opens in a new tab) to keep the design focused on what nurses need to do when AI shows up in their work. The approach starts with a goal, looks at what might get in the way, and builds in practice. Concepts and explanations support those actions rather than drive the course outline.

Our co-design findings guided the first version of the map. As Senior Learning Experience Designer, I facilitated working sessions in Miro with the instructors and project team, and we built the map together.

04 · Learning design

How each course builds AI literacy

The map led to the learning objectives for each course and to the framing we use throughout: four ways AI shows up in a nurse’s workflow. Below, you can explore the learning goals and activities for each course.

The learning progression and objectives

I drew on survey data from working nurses and conversations with partners to draft the progression, then shared it early for feedback. Nursing continuing education requirements also helped guide the learning objectives and design decisions.

Up to 9 credit hoursContinuing education for nurses through Duke University Health System Clinical Education and Professional Development, accredited by ANCC, ACPE, and ACCME.
Learners will be able to
Course 1: Seeing AI in Nursing PracticeRecognizewhere AI appears in nursing practice
  • Identify common AI tools used in nursing workflows.
  • Explain how AI systems transform clinical data into outputs.
  • Distinguish between rule-based systems, machine learning models, predictive AI, and generative AI.
  • Describe how healthcare organizations select and govern AI tools.
Course 2: Evaluating AI in Nursing PracticeEvaluatewhat an AI tool’s outputs are telling you
  • Explain how AI models learn from historical healthcare data.
  • Interpret common AI outputs with appropriate confidence and skepticism.
  • Identify situations where AI tools may perform poorly.
  • Recognize how bias can emerge from historical data and healthcare system patterns.
Course 3: Applying AI in Nursing PracticePracticeusing that understanding in clinical situations
  • Apply the AI-SBAR framework to interpret and communicate AI-informed clinical concerns.
  • Integrate AI outputs with patient assessment, clinical context, patient goals, and nursing judgment.
  • Respond appropriately when AI outputs align with or conflict with bedside observations.

05 · Learner testing

How testing with nurses refined the courses

By this point, what we were teaching was set. Testing with four practicing nurses shaped how we teach it and why it matters to them. They tried the terms we planned to use, a case scenario, and a demo of the Dialogue activity.

What we testedWhat nurses showed usWhat we changed
What we testedOur course vocabulary, such as inputs, outputs, and structured data.
What nurses showed usWe asked twice whether these terms matched their work, and both times the room went quiet. One nurse explained that when AI shows up on her unit, it is just called an alert.
What we changedTerm spotlights now introduce each term through a clinical example in the voice of an experienced nurse, and we rewrote the support materials in the words nurses used.
What we testedCases built around the care setting and the patient.
What nurses showed usEach nurse worked with a different record system and different risk scores, so the conversation kept returning to the tool in front of them.
What we changedEach course ends with a capstone about a tool from the nurse’s own workflow, and we wrote the activities so they don’t depend on any one product.
What we testedA scenario where a deterioration alert fires mid-shift.
What nurses showed usExperienced nurses said they check the patient first. Their worry was newer nurses, who may not yet know what to check.
What we changedThe checking steps they described became a reference checklist: compare the score with the patient, compare it with earlier readings, and confirm the data was entered and sent correctly.
What we testedA demo of the AI-powered Dialogue activity.
What nurses showed usAll four nurses said it would help. One asked what it would offer her and how it would count toward the course.
What we changedDialogue activities are optional and ungraded, and each one opens by saying what the nurse will get from it.

07 · Early results

What we know so far

AI for Nurses launched in September 2026, so the data is still coming in. Here’s what I can see so far.

Early data suggests the courses are well aligned and meeting the needs of nurses and administrators. That comes from partner feedback at our two-month review and from registered nurses’ responses in both pilot and live course surveys.

5.0

out of 5

Learners who completed a course or the full series rated it 5.0 out of 5, and their comments mention both the content and the experience.

Across all three courses, learners have flagged only a handful of items, and none pointed to a real problem.

Our partners funded a second year.

Over the next few months, I’ll be watching learner drop-off, item flags, and quiz question fidelity.

08 · Next steps

Continuing to improve the courses

The live courses launched without custom interactive activities while we evaluate other parts of the curriculum and the AI literacy competencies. As we learn more about learners’ workforce goals and how well the Specialization aligns with them, we’ll begin adding more interactive activities that work well in Coursera.
Proof of conceptThe Score and the Bedside
I built this prototype to explore how existing course materials could become more active practice in Coursera. What I’d figure out next

This is a proof of concept, not a finished course experience. Before using it with learners, I’d want to work through three things with the team.

  • AccessibilityTest keyboard navigation, screen reader support, and other access needs within Coursera, then work out what needs to change or have an alternative.
  • Learner dataDecide what would be useful to know about learners’ choices, progress, and engagement. Embedding it as ungraded practice might be a starting point, but we’d need to check what data we could collect for improvement.
  • LTI and tradeoffsExplore Coursera’s LTI support to see whether it could help with integration and reporting. I’d also look at which parts benefit from a simulation and where simpler resources, or a mix of both, might serve learners better.
