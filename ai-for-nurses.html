AI for Nurses

Helping nurses make sense of AI in their everyday work, and keep their own clinical judgment at the center of every decision.
Explore the learning examples ↓
At a glance

AI for Nurses is a three-course Coursera Specialization from the Duke University School of Nursing, designed for people across the nursing care team. I led the design and delivery with three faculty, with nurses helping shape the examples and activities along the way. It launched in September 2026. So far, learners who have finished rated it 5.0 out of 5, and our partners have funded a second year.

01 · A gap between AI learning and clinical practice

Making sense of AI on a nursing shift

Faculty at the Duke University School of Nursing were noticing a gap: nurses were being asked to use AI-enabled tools without much explanation of what they do or how to work with them. Sometimes a new tool appeared in their workflow with little introduction. Other times, AI was already part of the systems they used, but nurses had no reason to recognize it as AI.

Together with three School of Nursing faculty, we designed the series to give nurses a foundation for understanding and working with AI. It was the first of three planned Coursera Specializations.

02 · Co-designing with nurses

Learning where AI shows up across a real shift

During development, I designed and led a co-design session with registered nurses. They shared examples of how AI had shaped their recent shifts, reviewed our draft modules, and tried a demo of the AI-powered Dialogue activity. Four themes stood out and helped shape the Specialization.
AI is part of the workflow, even when it isn’t called AI.Nurses described alerts, scores, and automated summaries as part of the electronic health record (EHR), rather than as AI tools. Many arrived with little explanation of how they worked.
An alert is a reason to look closer, not an instruction.Nurses talked about checking an alert against what they saw in the patient. They noted that experienced nurses may trust their own assessment more, while newer nurses may lean more heavily on the number.
The tool doesn’t see everything a nurse sees.Nurses pointed to things they notice at the bedside that may not appear in the structured data behind a risk score, including nonverbal cues, behavior, and the patient’s environment.
A shorter note can leave out part of the story.AI-written summaries could save time, but nurses worried about losing assessment details the rest of the care team needed.

Turning what we learned into competencies

After the session, I drafted new competencies, the skills we wanted nurses to leave with, and compared them with the objectives we already had. Here’s where four of them ended up.
Proposed after the sessionIn the final courses
Describe what AI tools can’t capture, like nonverbal cues.Course 3 cases ask nurses to separate what the model could see from what existed only in their own observations and notes.
Evaluate and revise AI-generated documentation.A Course 3 telehealth case has nurses find what an AI summary missed and decide what the care team needs to know.
Communicate AI findings using SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation).Course 3 is built around AI-SBAR, an adaptation of the SBAR format nurses already use to share patient updates. Nurses practice documenting, communicating, and escalating concerns about an AI output.
Respond to alert fatigue and system friction.Course 2 explains that frequent alerts often come from a tool set up to catch as many at-risk patients as possible, which also means more false alarms. Course 3 has nurses practice talking with a provider who has stopped responding to alerts.

What learner testing informed for development

Testing with nurses improved the courses in three ways.

The cases.Our first set of case studies assumed a bedside role. Nurses who work away from the bedside told us those cases didn’t fit their day, so we adapted several of them.
The framing.Nurses described AI as something flagged, summarized, prioritized, or triggered. Those four patterns became the backbone of all three courses.
The support materials.We rewrote them to match the language nurses used.
03 · Key decisions

Three design decisions to support workforce learning

Early decisions that framed the foundational materials, like scripts and some readings, came from faculty expertise and an initial needs assessment survey. The co-design session confirmed those decisions and gave us concrete ways to build activities around nurses’ real workflows. These three decisions set the shape of all three courses, and the action map below shows how each part of a shift turned into specific practice.
  1. Organize every course around four patterns nurses already see.Nurses described AI as something flagged, summarized, prioritized, or triggered. Each course comes back to those four patterns, so new terms always connect to something nurses have seen at work.
  2. Build from seeing, to evaluating, to applying.Course 1 helps nurses spot where AI shows up in their work. Course 2 helps them judge what an output is telling them. Course 3 has them use that judgment in clinical cases.
  3. Pair short expert videos with practice at a clinical moment.Faculty videos run under seven minutes. In Course 3, case videos pause three times so nurses make a clinical call in the moment. Each course ends with a capstone based on the nurse’s own workflow.

05 · Learning design

How each course builds AI literacy

With the map in hand, we shaped three courses that build on each other. Here’s how they progress, how each one connects to what nurses see on a shift, and the activities inside them.

The learning progression and objectives

Survey data from working nurses and conversations with partners shaped a progression I shared early with stakeholders. Because the Specialization is accredited for nursing continuing education, accreditation requirements also informed design decisions along the way.

Up to 9 credit hoursContinuing education for nurses through Duke University Health System Clinical Education and Professional Development, accredited by ANCC, ACPE, and ACCME.
Learners will be able to
Course 1: Seeing AI in Nursing PracticeRecognizewhere AI appears in nursing practice
  • Identify common AI tools used in nursing workflows.
  • Explain how AI systems transform clinical data into outputs.
  • Distinguish between rule-based systems, machine learning models, predictive AI, and generative AI.
  • Describe how healthcare organizations select and govern AI tools.
Course 2: Evaluating AI in Nursing PracticeEvaluatewhat an AI tool’s outputs are telling you
  • Explain how AI models learn from historical healthcare data.
  • Interpret common AI outputs with appropriate confidence and skepticism.
  • Identify situations where AI tools may perform poorly.
  • Recognize how bias can emerge from historical data and healthcare system patterns.
Course 3: Applying AI in Nursing PracticePracticeusing that understanding in clinical situations
  • Apply the AI-SBAR framework to interpret and communicate AI-informed clinical concerns.
  • Integrate AI outputs with patient assessment, clinical context, patient goals, and nursing judgment.
  • Respond appropriately when AI outputs align with or conflict with bedside observations.

Seven activity types and the capstones

Lessons pair a short expert video with readings that build vocabulary and connect it to real tools, then check understanding along the way.
Explore
Check
Apply
ExploreExpert videosDuke School of Nursing faculty introduce concepts in clinical language, in short videos. In Course 3, case study videos pause three times so nurses make a real-time clinical judgment.
ExploreTerm spotlightsShort readings on one term or a closely related pair, written in the voice of an experienced nurse mentor and grounded in a clinical example. The goal is shared language nurses can use with each other on the unit.
ExploreInside the AI toolReadings that unpack a specific clinical tool, such as the Epic Deterioration Index or the Braden Scale, with a consistent structure for what it can and cannot see.
CheckPractice quizzesUngraded checkpoints within a lesson, typically after a reading or before a video.
CheckGraded quizzesApplication-level questions with unlimited attempts and feedback on every answer option, correct and incorrect.
ApplyDialogue activitiesDialogue is a Coursera activity where learners have a back-and-forth conversation with Coursera’s AI coach. I set the objectives, instructions, and rubric for each one, and the coach draws on that module’s videos and readings to ask questions and respond. Conversations stay private to the learner.The capstone in each course
Course 1Seeing AI in Your WorkflowNurses identify and describe an AI tool from their own practice.
Course 2Evaluating AI in Your WorkflowNurses bring a tool they’ve had questions about and evaluate it.
Course 3Applying AI in Your WorkflowNurses bring a clinical moment where an AI output shaped a decision.
ApplyPost-case reflectionsAfter each Course 3 case, learners get a case summary and a sample reflection that names the tool and output, what the model had and couldn’t see, the gap, and what the nurse noticed.
