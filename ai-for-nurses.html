AI for Nurses

Helping nurses make sense of AI in their everyday work.

I led learning design and project management for a three-course Coursera Specialization developed with Duke School of Nursing and Johnson & Johnson. We built the series to help nurses recognize AI in their work, consider what a tool is telling them, and bring their own clinical judgment to the decision.

Where we started

Nurses were already encountering AI tools during their shifts. We wanted the courses to help them understand those tools through situations they knew and questions that mattered to their work.

I began with survey data collected from working nurses and conversations with our project partners. That helped me propose a three-course path: first recognizing where AI appears, then evaluating its outputs, and finally practicing how to use that understanding in clinical situations.

  1. Course 1Recognizewhere AI appears in nursing practice
  2. Course 2Evaluatewhat an AI tool’s outputs are telling you
  3. Course 3Practiceusing that understanding in clinical situations

I shared the proposed structure and early learning examples with stakeholders so we could agree on the direction before developing the courses further.

What we learned from nurses

We brought registered nurses into the design process to help us understand how the learning would fit their experience.

In one session, they described four ways AI showed up during a shift: something was flagged, summarized, prioritized, or triggered. Those descriptions gave us a familiar way to organize the content.

How the learning comes together

Each module introduces a concept, gives learners a way to explore it, and connects it to nursing practice.

Subject-matter expert videos explain ideas in clinical language. Short Term Spotlights make individual terms more concrete. Interactive activities give nurses a chance to try an idea and see what happens.

Practice assessments, conversations about tools from learners’ own workplaces, and final assessments support the progression across the series. Videos, readings, activities, and assessments are all designed with accessibility at the forefront. I used ChatGPT and Claude, along with Claude Code, to support the design of the courses, including the activities below.

1. Comparing two kinds of AI

In this activity, learners explore a fall risk score and a generated discharge summary. They can run the examples again and observe what changes.

The activity introduces predictive and generative AI through familiar situations and gives learners a reason to look more closely at the information each tool produces.

2. Exploring what a model uses

Another activity lets learners change the information a model receives and observe how its output responds.

It gives learners a way to explore inputs and outputs while considering what the model may know, and what a nurse may know from the wider situation.

3. Practicing a clinical decision

The third course brings the ideas together through five guided clinical cases. Learners review a situation, pause during a video to make a decision, and reflect on their reasoning.

These cases give nurses a chance to practice applying the course concepts in the context of patient care.

Bringing the project together

Alongside learning design, I led project management as we moved through writing, recording, activity development, learner testing, and partner review.

The work involved faculty, nurses, our design team, and project partners. Early examples and opportunities for feedback helped us build a shared understanding of what we were creating.

The Specialization launched in September 2026. Duke School of Nursing and Johnson & Johnson funded a second year of collaboration, and we’re now developing a new Specialization that builds on what we learned together.
