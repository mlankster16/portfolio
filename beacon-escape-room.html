Light the Beacon

A virtual escape room for Grade 6 math, where ratio reasoning brings a storm-damaged lighthouse back online.
Play the escape room ↓
At a glance

The idea came from conversations with my middle school students years ago. I designed the learning sequence, practice, and feedback, built the playable prototype with AI tools, and created a two-day classroom unit to go with it. It hasn’t been tested with students yet, but it’s what I’d want to try with them if I were back in the classroom.

01 · An idea from my classroom

Ratios and rates in a two-day unit

It’s built for Grade 6, early in the ratio unit, after ratio tables and before percents. It covers Common Core 6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.2, 6.RP.A.3a, and 6.RP.A.3b over two days on a block schedule, or four 45-minute periods. The game runs in the browser on Chromebooks and iPads, with no logins or downloads.

03 · Key decisions

A few choices behind the game

In the game
  1. Give learners something to work with.Learners build a ratio with tiles, extend a ratio table, and use a radio log to figure out how many beeps happen in one minute. I wanted them to see and work with the math rather than just entering an answer (they do this, too!).
  2. Make it easy to ask for help.Every room has an optional three-part practice and three hints that get more specific along the way. There’s no penalty for using them.
  3. Ask how they know it works.In the signal gallery, learners use their logbook clues to set the dials and test the signal. Then they use their own numbers to finish a sentence explaining why it works.
Around the game
  1. Intentional framing and designated exploration.The Keeper’s Logbook introduces the vocabulary and mission on Day 1, so students have a chance to get familiar with both before entering the escape room.
  2. Design for pairs finishing at different times.Each lesson is designed to fit one class period. Early finishers can start designing their own room. Those who need longer can pick up where they left off at the start of the next lesson. The game saves progress in the browser, and a five-letter room code lets a pair keep going on a different Chromebook or iPad.
  3. Have a plan for who might miss out.Driver and Navigator roles give both partners a job. Paper Rooms offer another way to take part for students who can’t use the game, and there’s a make-up plan for anyone who’s absent. Teacher notes also suggest adaptations for Grades 5 and 7.
  4. Save time to talk about their thinking.For Lesson 3, I’ve planned a discussion where students compare strategies and work through possible mistakes, such as “14 flashes,” rather than recap the story. Once I’ve tried it with a class, I’d use examples from their work to guide that conversation.

05 · Learning design

Inside each room

Built on Common Core 6.RP.A.3 (opens in a new tab) ↗, mainly 3a (equivalent ratios and ratio tables) and 3b (unit rates). Each room pairs one part of the standard with a hands-on choice, an optional practice round, three hints, and feedback for each kind of mistake.

Feedback for a common mistake A learner answers 12 blue, 8 gold: So close, but the colors are switched. You have 3 blue for every 2 gold.
The taskFill a 20-spot lens so it keeps 2 blue for every 3 gold, counting the 4 gold panels already in place.
The math6.RP.A.3a: Equivalent ratios“I can build equivalent ratios and complete a ratio table.”How can two different-looking ratios describe the same relationship?
Hands-on choice: Check the groupsOnce the lens lights up, learners say how many groups of 5 fit in the 20 spots, then pick the strategy that matches their thinking: equal groups, part of the whole, or guess and check.
Practice firstDescribe a ratio with “for every,” read and extend groups, then build a 10-spot practice lamp.
Hints, from a nudge to a next step
  1. 1Hint 1: What is the ratio of blue to gold? How many lights are in one group of that ratio?
  2. 2Hint 2: The lens has 20 spots. How many groups of 5 is that?
  3. 3Hint 3: Four groups of 2 blue and 3 gold is how many of each? The 4 stuck gold panels count too.
Feedback for a common mistake A learner answers 14 flashes: Close. When the packs doubled from 4 to 8, the flashes doubled too. Did your answer grow the same way?
The taskComplete a ratio table: 4 packs make 6 flashes, so 10 packs make 15.
The math6.RP.A.3a: Equivalent ratios“I can build equivalent ratios and complete a ratio table.”How can two different-looking ratios describe the same relationship?
Hands-on choice: Pick a way to startLearners can start from a row they know or from the flashes for 1 pack. After the generator runs, they pick the strategy they used.
Practice firstBuild a tape diagram by repeating a group, then fill a practice table with a different ratio.
Hints, from a nudge to a next step
  1. 1Hint 1: Look at each row. How do packs and flashes change together?
  2. 2Hint 2: 4 packs make 6 flashes. What would 2 packs make?
  3. 3Hint 3: 10 packs is 8 packs plus 2 packs. Add those flashes together.
Feedback for a common mistake A learner answers 3 beeps a minute: At 3 a minute, 3 minutes only make 9 beeps. The log shows 18. Share the 18 beeps evenly across the 3 minutes.
The taskFind the unit rate of 6 beeps per minute, then use it: 42 beeps take 7 minutes.
The math6.RP.A.3b: Unit rates“I can find a unit rate and use it to solve a problem.”Why is it useful to know what happens in one unit?
Hands-on choice: Fill in the radio logLearners find the beeps in 1 minute, then the minutes for all 42 beeps. After the radio comes on, they pick the strategy they used.
Practice firstSplit 20 beeps into equal parts to see the unit rate, then use it on a practice buoy.
Hints, from a nudge to a next step
  1. 1Hint 1: 18 beeps over 3 minutes. Every minute gets the same number of beeps.
  2. 2Hint 2: Share 18 beeps evenly across 3 minutes: 18 ÷ 3.
  3. 3Hint 3: Count up by 6 for each minute until you reach 42.
Feedback for a common mistake A learner answers 15 flashes: The beacon flickers and dims. 15 flashes would need more than the 8 power packs you have left. 10 packs made 15 flashes, so how many do 8 packs make?
The taskA storm cracks the lens, leaving 15 usable spots, knocks out 2 power packs, and lengthens the message to 36 beeps. Learners reuse what they found in each room to set three dials, one at a time, then test the signal and explain why it works.
The math6.RP.A.3a–b: Reasoning in context“I can use ratios and rates to choose a plan and explain why it works.”How do ratios and rates help us make a decision?
Hands-on choice: Explain itLearners fill in a sentence with their own numbers: the packs their signal uses and the packs they have, the time the message takes and the time before the ship arrives.
Practice firstCheck plans against limits, then finish a sentence that explains why a practice signal works.
Hints, from a nudge to a next step
  1. 1Hint 1: Start with what changed. Your limits are listed above the steps.
  2. 2Hint 2: Break each one into equal groups. 20 lens spots was 4 groups of 5, so 15 spots is 3 groups. 10 packs was 5 groups of 2 packs, so 8 packs is 4 groups.
  3. 3Hint 3: Each group of 5 spots has 2 blue and 3 gold. Each group of 2 packs makes 3 flashes. At 6 beeps a minute, 36 beeps take 36 ÷ 6 minutes.

In every room, learners can type answers instead of turning the dials.

06 · In the classroom

Two days at Gull Point

Here’s how a teacher would bring the escape room into class. I modeled the teacher guide on the Engineering is Elementary guides from the Museum of Science, Boston: a story sets up the problem, partners practice the tools, then they put those tools to work and talk about what they learned.

Day 1: Story

The Keeper’s Logbook

An illustrated short story told across eight slides. Sixth grader Mira visits her grandfather Pop, the keeper of Gull Point Light. As he shows her the lens, his logbook, the generator, and the radio, students hear each math word as it comes up. Then a storm knocks out the light, and students become Mira’s crew. Like the storybooks in Engineering is Elementary, it sets up the problem, but it’s written with sixth graders in mind.

07 · Measuring impact

How I’d evaluate its impact

Before the escape room, learners answer two quick questions about lanterns. Afterward, they answer the same two questions with new numbers. Each time, they also say how sure they are. Comparing the rounds shows whether learners can use ratios and rates on their own, and whether their confidence matches their skill. In class, the after round is the Lantern Check in Lesson 3 (handout 3-3). Step through three sample learners below.
