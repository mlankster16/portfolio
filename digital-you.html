Let’s Talk Digital You

Technical and Ethical Aspects of a Data-Centric World: a for-credit, interdisciplinary course open to students in any major.
See inside the course ↓
At a glance

Let’s Talk Digital You is a for-credit, interdisciplinary Duke course in the University Course series, open to undergraduates in any major, mostly in their first year. It explored the technical and ethical sides of a data-centric world as a hybrid, semester-long course in Canvas. As Senior Learning Experience Designer and Project Manager, I led the planning, curriculum mapping, and design strategy with two lead instructors, and managed the project from April 2023 through the debrief in May 2024.

Week 4A Technical Perspective on I Made Generative ArtLecture · January 29, 2024

What transforms when the brushstrokes of a canvas are encoded into pixels on a screen? How does the essence of visual culture morph as we shift from the analog to the digital? This week, we will discuss the evolution of visual culture, probing the dichotomy between the physicality of analog and the abstraction of digital media.

By the end of this week, you will be able toCompare the characteristics of analog and digital media, using examples to explain how each medium shapes viewers’ perceptions and an artwork’s cultural value.Explain how an analog artwork is converted into a digital format, identifying what changes in its materiality, scale, and representation of visual information.Identify how neural networks and Stable Diffusion represent and process visual data.Describe how artists use these methods to create new forms of visual expression.
  1. Before class
    No readings or pre-class assignments this week.
  2. In class
    Lecture by a professor of the practice in Art, Art History & Visual Studies and a technology company co-founder, followed by the lab in EdStem with your small group.
  3. After classDue Feb 2
    Draft a 300–500 word technical reflection, share it in your section’s discussion, then take the weekly self-assessment.
Week 4 · LabText-to-image toolsEdStem · In class, led by TAs
Overview · 1 of 6

In this lab you will work through a series of exercises in which you will explore how text-to-image tools like Stable Diffusion work. As you work through these exercises, you will be asked to consider a range of thought-provoking questions that will help you develop a better appreciation of the kinds of relationships between text and image that these models have learned.

By the end of this lab, you will be able toDescribe the complex relationships between text prompts and output images in the case of text-to-image toolsFormulate more critical questions about dataset curation and its real-world effectsExplain the importance of labeling
Lab formatYour TA will lead you through the information on the following pages in a live lecture/discussion format. A text version is provided so students can think ahead about the questions and review the material throughout the semester.
Training data · 2 of 6“Ceci n’est pas une pipe” – René Magritte

The ability to translate text into visual images is a remarkable recent achievement. In the following series of activities, you will explore the ability of a state-of-the-art text-to-image tool to create realistic images using only text prompts. By critically evaluating the output, you will try to develop a better understanding of the kinds of relationships between text and images that the model has learned.

An important (and often uncelebrated) reason for the success of recent AI models is the creation of gargantuan training datasets. For example, the LAION-400-MILLION open dataset has been used to train various text-to-image models. As the name indicates, this is a massive dataset made up of 400 million image-text pairs.

Key questionA model is only as good as the data used to train it. But if a training dataset becomes so big that it’s composed of literally hundreds of millions or even billions of examples, how do we ensure the quality of each example?

Test for bias · 4 of 6

In this section you will experiment with the Stable Diffusion tool on the Hugging Face website. Design and enter a prompt to generate an image depicting a specific profession or role, in an important action.

Prompt patternA [profession] [performing an appropriate action]
“A doctor consulting a patient”“A landscaper trimming a tree”“An inmate exercising in prison”“A sanitation worker cleaning a toilet”
Questions to guide your experimentationWhile keeping the profession type are you able to change the representation of the figure?What words can you use to move away from stereotypes presented in the original image?How do images perpetuate cultural stereotypes?Who selected the images used to train this AI?What alternative images exist but may not be discoverable, shared or incorporated into training data sets?What does representation look like on the internet?
Make art · 5 of 6

In this section you will test the ability of generative AI to create compelling, important artwork images. Using Stable Diffusion study the creation of emotive artwork.

01EmotionCan you create an image that conveys emotion?What is emotion, and how do you work through prompts to create it?
02BeautyCan you create an image that has Beauty?What is beauty?
03CritiqueCan you create an image that critiques culture?How can imagery critique the political, economic or social condition?
Wrap up · 6 of 6

In this lab we have discussed the practical problems associated with creating gigantic training datasets and the subjective challenges of deciding what the labels should even be, a problem which is especially acute in the case of labeling works of art. In this final activity, you will go through an interactive essay which explores the challenges of deciding what the labels should be, even for ostensibly “simple” images.

Week 5An Ethical Perspective on I Made Generative ArtLecture · February 5, 2024

How are we as humans to think about the advent of AI-generated art? Is it “art” in the sense of the word to which we are accustomed? If not, why not? This week, we will discuss the social, ethical and human implications of the latest development in the evolution of visual culture that we began discussing last week.

By the end of this week, you will be able toEvaluate whether AI-generated works meet different definitions and purposes of art, supporting your position with examples and responding to an alternative perspective.Analyze how AI-generated art challenges ideas about human creativity, authorship, and identity, using specific examples to explain its social and ethical implications.

Week 5Generative AI and Art: Ethics HypotheticalCase study · In class, in small groups led by TAs

As part of an effort to increase undergraduate interest in their programming, the Nasher Museum of Art is hosting a six-month exhibition that will be curated in collaboration with Duke students. The exhibition, entitled “Embodied Souls: Human Narratives and Artistic Expression,” is meant to explore the ways in which art can consciously and unconsciously convey the values, emotions, and perspectives of artists. The curatorial staff has shortlisted ten works to be included in this exhibition, and they have asked you to serve on a student panel to provide input on their final decision.

Reflecting the Nasher Museum’s ongoing interest in the intersection of art and technology, the shortlist includes works in a variety of artistic media, including several created using generative AI.

02 · Key decisions

Three design decisions to support learning

  1. Pair each technology with its ethics.The design challenge was cohesion: one integrated course instead of disconnected mini-lectures. Each theme ran across two weeks, first how a technology works, then its ethical implications, with two panel weeks for live Q&A across disciplines.
  2. Give every week the same six-step rhythm.With the lead instructors, I defined five course outcomes, then mapped every week to the same structure, from lecture and small-group lab or case study to reflection, co-curricular, and self-assessment, so guests and TAs could begin co-design work.
  3. Build choice into every step.Universal Design for Learning shaped each step. Students could opt out of labs on ethical grounds, wrote reflections with no set prompt and could use multimedia, and chose their own co-curricular events.

About the project and teaching team▾

University Courses are university-wide, interdisciplinary courses that help undergraduates engage with pressing issues of our time, such as race and climate change. Let’s Talk Digital You was the next course in the series. It focused on technology, ethics, and identity, and it was open to students in any major with no prerequisites.

I led the high-level planning, curriculum mapping, and design strategy in close partnership with the two lead instructors, and I managed the project from April 2023 through the course debrief in May 2024. Once the framework was in place, the Learning Experience Designers on my team built the course and ran QA, and the media team worked with faculty who chose to develop multimedia content.

Course outcomes and the weekly rhythm▾
With the lead instructors, I defined five course outcomes, then mapped every week to the same six-step structure so guests and TAs could begin co-design work. Universal Design for Learning, Duke-supported tools, and facilitation guides were built into each step.
From the syllabus · by the end of this course, students will be able to
1Outcome 1: Develop a high-level understanding of various emerging technologies, valuing an interdisciplinary lens.
2Outcome 2: Evaluate emerging technologies through the lens of technical and ethical concepts (including algorithmic bias, privacy, societal impact) to discern how to respond to future developments.
3Outcome 3: Describe how technologies, and you using certain technologies, have societal and personal implications.
4Outcome 4: Analyze the benefits and costs of emerging technologies on both society and your personal life.
5Outcome 5: Consider how technology specifically forms and shapes communities and the impacts of this on society.
