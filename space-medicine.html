Space Medicine

A Duke course on Coursera that I managed and designed, plus an interactive concept that reimagines Week 2.
Explore the reimagined Week 2 ↓
At a glance

Space Medicine has reached nearly 30,000 learners since 2021. For this portfolio, I reimagined Week 2 as a more interactive experience, with a flight surgeon mentor and an activity after every video. The redesign is a concept, and this page shows what I kept, what I changed, and how I’d test it.

01 · From live course to concept

What if Week 2 were built for interaction?

Space Medicine launched on Coursera in 2021 on a tight timeline. As Project Manager and Senior Learning Experience Designer, I coordinated the media teams and guided script development.

Week 2 is mostly video: 24 minutes, followed by a graded quiz and an optional reflection. For this concept, I kept its structure and videos and redesigned what happens around them, so learners put each idea to use as soon as they meet it.

03 · Knowledge graph

How Week 2’s ideas fit together

Space Medicine is about understanding how the body, physics, and engineering connect, rather than preparing for a specific job. A knowledge graph helped us map those connections: what learners need to understand first, which ideas return later, and where they might run into misconceptions.

We built the course’s graph collaboratively in Miro with the instructors and project team. As Senior Learning Experience Designer, I facilitated the mapping and co-designed the graph.

04 · Key decisions

Three design decisions to support learning

  1. Keep the videos and integrate real-world relevance.Learners watch the same 24 minutes of video as in the live course. The redesign adds 14 minutes of mentor chats and five short activities.
  2. Put each idea to use right away.Each video leads into a hands-on activity. Learners then bring those ideas together to make decisions in a real-time launch-day scenario.
  3. Give the week a story and a mentor.Dr. Elena Rivera, a lead flight surgeon, guides learners through chats as they prepare for a high-altitude flight and investigate the real 1862 balloon flight.

06 · Measuring impact

Does the redesign make a difference?

Learners in the live course pass the Week 2 quiz, and the course has a 4.8 rating. Those are encouraging signs, but they don’t tell me whether this redesign would be better. Would learners stay with it, enjoy it more, and learn at least as well? Would the difference justify the time spent building it?

If Coursera supports random assignment for this test, I’d split new learners between two versions of Week 2.

ALive Week 2
  • The existing 24 minutes of video
  • The existing Week 2 quiz
BReimagined Week 2
  • The same 24 minutes of video
  • Dr. Rivera’s chats (only in this version)
  • Five short activities (only in this version)
  • The launch-day scenario (only in this version)
  • The same Week 2 quiz
In both versionsOnly in Version BSee both versions block by block ↑

Keeping the videos and quiz the same gives us a common starting point. The comparison would test the added experience as a whole, including the extra time learners spend on it.

What I’d measure

1. EngagementMain questionDo more learners finish the week and keep going?I’d compare Week 2 completion, where learners leave, and how many start Week 3.
2. SatisfactionMain questionDo learners find the week more engaging and worthwhile?I’d compare item ratings and ask both groups: “How much did this week hold your attention?” and “What will you remember from it?”
3. LearningCheckDo learners perform at least as well on the quiz?I’d compare Week 2 quiz scores and first-try pass rates. Better engagement wouldn’t be enough if learning suffered.
4. Lasting learning, if feasibleCan learners apply the ideas later?I’d give both groups a new hypoxia scenario later in the course. This would help check whether any benefit lasts beyond the end-of-week quiz.

How I’d decide

Before running the test, I’d define what would count as a worthwhile improvement and how much evidence we’d need.

  • Keep Version B if engagement or satisfaction improves meaningfully without a meaningful drop in quiz performance.
  • Revise and test again if the results are mixed. Activity data and learner feedback would help me decide what to change.
  • Stay with Version A if the redesign adds time without a clear benefit. That would be useful to know, too.
If too few learners take part to draw a clear conclusion, I’d treat the results as an early pilot and use interviews to help plan the next test.

What I’d look at inside Version B

Comparing the two versions would tell me about the week as a whole. A closer look at the activities would help me figure out what needs work.
